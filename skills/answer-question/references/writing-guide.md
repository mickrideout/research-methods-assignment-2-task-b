# Writing Style Guidelines: Natural, Human-Like Academic Writing

## 1. Objective

Write answers in the style of a competent postgraduate student who understands the subject, has engaged critically with the relevant material, and can explain their reasoning clearly.

The writing must be academically rigorous, factually accurate, specific to the assignment, and aligned with the marking rubric. It should read as coherent academic prose rather than generic, formulaic text produced by a language model.

Prioritise, in order:

1. Correctness and fidelity to the evidence.
2. Complete coverage of the question and marking criteria.
3. Clear reasoning and subject-specific analysis.
4. Natural, concise and varied academic expression.
5. Consistent terminology, tone and formatting.

Never sacrifice accuracy, necessary detail or academic quality to achieve a particular writing style.

## 2. Understand the question before writing

Before drafting an answer:

- Identify the precise question being asked, its scope, and its command words (e.g. analyse, evaluate, compare, justify, discuss, design).
- Identify the knowledge, evidence, calculations, examples and reasoning required to answer it.
- Consult the assignment specification, enhanced rubric, requirements checklist, relevant course materials and other approved sources.
- Determine what the question requires the reader to understand or conclude.
- Establish a logical line of reasoning before composing prose.

Answer the actual question rather than producing a general overview of its subject area.

Do not write a generic introduction to the topic unless it establishes context necessary to answer the question. Do not explain concepts the intended academic reader is likely to know unless their explanation is relevant to the analysis.

## 3. Develop an argument, not a sequence of observations

Each answer should have a clear intellectual purpose. Build the response around the reasoning needed to satisfy the question.

- State the main finding, position or recommendation early when the question permits.
- Explain why the finding follows from the evidence, theory, calculation or analysis.
- Connect supporting points to the central argument.
- Distinguish established facts, interpretations, assumptions and recommendations.
- Explain meaningful relationships, trade-offs, limitations and implications.
- Where relevant, compare alternatives using explicit criteria rather than describing them separately.
- Make evaluative judgements using evidence and reasoning, not unsupported assertions.

Prefer a specific analytical statement over a broad claim followed by generic elaboration.

For example:

Weak:\
"Artificial intelligence has many advantages and disadvantages in the workplace. It is important to consider these factors when implementing AI systems."

Stronger:\
"Automated employee-risk predictions can help HR identify where support may be needed, but they can also encourage managers to treat uncertain predictions as established facts. Any implementation should therefore distinguish decision support from decisions about individual employees."

The stronger example identifies a concrete tension and explains its practical significance.

## 4. Use direct, natural academic prose

Write as though explaining a well-considered conclusion to an informed academic reader.

- Prefer familiar, precise words over unnecessarily elaborate vocabulary.
- Use the shortest phrasing that preserves the intended meaning and necessary nuance.
- Prefer active constructions when they clarify who does what.
- Use passive constructions when the process, result or object is more important than the actor, particularly in technical and scientific writing.
- Vary sentence length according to the complexity of the ideas.
- Use straightforward sentences for key findings and more complex sentences when relationships between ideas genuinely require them.
- Use discipline-appropriate terminology where it adds precision.
- Define specialist terminology when necessary for the intended audience.
- Use contractions sparingly in formal academic prose; follow the conventions of the discipline and assignment.

Do not deliberately introduce grammatical errors, awkward wording, slang, or informal expressions to make the writing seem more human.

## 5. Avoid repetitive and formulaic language

Do not repeatedly rely on the same sentence openings, paragraph structures, transitions or concluding phrases.

Avoid using the following expressions as habitual filler:

- "It is important to note that..."
- "It is worth noting that..."
- "Furthermore, it is important to consider..."
- "In today's rapidly evolving world..."
- "In the ever-changing landscape of..."
- "This highlights the importance of..."
- "This underscores the significance of..."
- "A key takeaway is that..."
- "Delving deeper into..."
- "At its core..."
- "It is crucial to understand that..."
- "In conclusion, it can be said that..."
- "Ultimately, this demonstrates that..."

These expressions are not prohibited when genuinely appropriate. Remove them when they add no information.

Prefer stating the substantive point directly.

Instead of:\
"It is important to note that the model has certain limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results."

Write:\
"The model's predictions are less reliable for groups underrepresented in the training data."

Avoid repeatedly beginning sentences with "This suggests", "This indicates", "This demonstrates" or "This highlights". State the implication directly where possible.

## 6. Eliminate unnecessary verbosity

Every sentence should contribute meaningfully to the answer.

- Remove statements that merely announce the topic.
- Do not repeat a claim in different words to create an impression of depth.
- Avoid restating the question before answering it.
- Remove background information that does not help answer the question.
- Avoid long lists of loosely related considerations when a smaller number of well-supported points is sufficient.
- Do not add a summary paragraph when the answer is already clear and the question does not require one.
- Avoid explaining the same concept in the introduction, body and conclusion without a clear purpose.
- Use headings only when they improve navigation or are appropriate to the assignment format.

Concision does not mean omitting necessary explanations. A technical claim may require several sentences to establish its basis, explain its implications and acknowledge its limitations.

Aim for high information density: each paragraph should advance the answer.

## 7. Construct paragraphs around meaningful ideas

Each paragraph should perform a distinct function in the argument.

A useful default structure is:

1. **Point:** Introduce the relevant claim or analytical observation.
2. **Support:** Provide the evidence, theory, example or calculation that supports it.
3. **Reasoning:** Explain how the support justifies the claim.
4. **Implication:** Connect the result to the question, where appropriate.

Treat this as a reasoning aid, not a rigid template. Do not force every paragraph into the same four-sentence pattern.

Vary paragraph length according to the complexity of the idea. A short paragraph can effectively state a key finding; a more complex argument may need several paragraphs.

Use transitions to express genuine logical relationships, such as contrast, causation, qualification or consequence. Do not insert transition words mechanically at the beginning of every paragraph.

## 8. Make analysis specific and substantive

Generic academic language is not a substitute for analysis.

Whenever relevant:

- Name the specific theory, method, variable, system, result or stakeholder being discussed.
- Refer to actual findings, numerical results, source material or assignment evidence.
- Explain the mechanism behind a relationship rather than merely stating that a relationship exists.
- Distinguish correlation from causation.
- Explain why an observed result matters in the context of the question.
- Identify assumptions that materially affect the conclusion.
- Compare competing explanations or approaches where the question requires evaluation.
- Acknowledge uncertainty and limitations in proportion to their significance.
- Draw conclusions that follow from the evidence, not conclusions that sound academically impressive.

Avoid generic statements such as "the results provide valuable insights" unless you explain precisely what the results reveal and why that matters.

When discussing code, algorithms, statistical models or technical systems, describe their actual behaviour and consequences rather than relying on broad claims about efficiency, scalability, robustness or effectiveness.

## 9. Use evidence naturally and responsibly

Integrate citations and source material into the reasoning rather than attaching references to generic claims.

- Attribute theories, empirical findings and non-obvious claims to the relevant sources.
- Explain how cited evidence supports the point being made.
- Compare sources when their findings, assumptions or conclusions differ.
- Distinguish source findings from your own interpretation of those findings.
- Use direct quotations sparingly unless textual analysis or exact wording is required.
- Never invent sources, citations, page numbers, quotations, results or evidence.
- Do not claim to have conducted an experiment, interview, literature search or data analysis unless the work was actually performed or documented in the supplied materials.
- Preserve the required referencing style and ensure citations correspond to the reference list.

Avoid using citations as decoration. A reference should support a claim, establish attribution or provide necessary context.

## 10. Express uncertainty precisely

Academic writing should be appropriately confident without overstating what the evidence establishes.

- State well-supported findings directly.
- Qualify conclusions when the evidence, sample size, assumptions or methodology warrants qualification.
- Distinguish demonstrated effects from plausible explanations or recommendations.
- Explain material limitations rather than appending generic disclaimers.
- Avoid excessive hedging that makes established findings sound uncertain.
- Do not use words such as "clearly", "obviously", "undoubtedly" or "proves" unless they are genuinely justified.

Prefer a specific qualification over vague caution.

For example:\
"The association is consistent with the proposed explanation, although the observational design does not establish causation."

This identifies the limitation and its consequence without weakening the finding unnecessarily.

## 11. Match the writing to the question type

Adapt the structure and tone to the task.

- **Explain:** Establish how or why something works, using a clear causal or conceptual sequence.
- **Analyse:** Examine relationships, mechanisms, evidence and implications.
- **Evaluate:** Apply explicit criteria, weigh strengths against weaknesses and reach a justified judgement.
- **Compare:** Establish meaningful points of comparison and explain their significance.
- **Discuss:** Develop a balanced argument, address relevant alternatives and reach a reasoned position.
- **Justify:** Connect the proposed choice to criteria, evidence and constraints.
- **Design or propose:** Present a feasible solution, explain the rationale and address relevant implementation trade-offs.
- **Calculate or solve:** Show the necessary method, assumptions, intermediate reasoning and result.
- **Reflect:** Analyse specific experiences or decisions, explain their significance and identify defensible learning outcomes.

Do not apply the same introduction-body-conclusion template to every question. Some questions are best answered with a short, direct explanation; others require a structured argument, table, calculation or technical artefact.

## 12. Use structure and formatting purposefully

Follow the assignment's formatting requirements first.

- Use headings when the answer is substantial or the rubric specifies distinct components.
- Use prose for connected reasoning and lists for genuinely discrete items.
- Use tables when they improve comparison, summarisation or traceability.
- Use equations, code blocks and figures where the task benefits from them.
- Avoid excessive headings that fragment a short answer.
- Do not use a bullet list to avoid explaining a complex relationship.
- Avoid repeating the question as a heading unless required or useful for navigation.
- Keep terminology, notation, tense and abbreviations consistent.

Formatting should make the reasoning easier to follow, not make a thin answer appear more substantial.

## 13. Preserve an authentic academic voice

Use a consistent voice appropriate to the discipline and the assessment.

- Make the reasoning visible through specific explanations and justified judgements.
- Prefer concrete observations over generic claims about what researchers, organisations or practitioners should do.
- Use first person only when appropriate to the assignment, particularly reflective or explicitly personal tasks.
- Do not invent personal experiences, opinions, observations or practical work to create a personal voice.
- Do not imitate a particular author's distinctive style unless explicitly permitted and appropriate.
- Do not force humour, anecdotes, rhetorical questions or conversational language into formal academic answers.
- Allow the complexity of the subject to determine the complexity of the writing.

The objective is not to simulate a particular person. It is to produce clear, considered writing in which the substance of the analysis determines how the answer is expressed.

## 14. Maintain assignment and pipeline consistency

Because answers are generated question by question, apply these additional rules across the full assignment.

- Read the shared assignment configuration and relevant preceding answers before drafting when available.
- Reuse established definitions, notation and assumptions consistently.
- Avoid repeating explanations already supplied in another answer unless the current question requires them.
- Cross-reference earlier answers when useful and permitted by the assignment format.
- Maintain a consistent academic register without making every answer sound structurally identical.
- Preserve the required level of technical detail and depth for each question.
- Respect question-specific word limits and the overall assignment word budget.
- Do not invent connections between questions merely to create narrative continuity.
- If a later answer contradicts an earlier answer, investigate and resolve the inconsistency rather than silently preserving it.
- Keep factual claims traceable to sources, calculations, data or documented assumptions.

Variation should arise from differences in the questions and the reasoning required, not from randomly changing the tone.

## 15. Revision and quality-control procedure

After drafting each answer, perform a separate style review.

### Pass 1: Question alignment

- Does the answer address every part of the question?
- Are the command word and marking criteria satisfied?
- Is the main finding or argument clear?

### Pass 2: Substantive quality

- Are the claims supported by evidence or sound reasoning?
- Are the assumptions, calculations and technical details correct?
- Are material limitations and alternative explanations addressed where relevant?
- Are any claims stronger than the evidence permits?

### Pass 3: Natural expression

- Can any sentence be expressed more directly without losing meaning?
- Are any phrases generic, repetitive or unnecessarily elaborate?
- Do several consecutive sentences follow the same pattern?
- Are transitions expressing real logical relationships?
- Does each paragraph advance the answer?
- Does the response sound like a considered explanation rather than a sequence of generic academic statements?

### Pass 4: Assignment-wide consistency

- Is terminology consistent with the rest of the assignment?
- Is any material repeated unnecessarily?
- Are cross-references accurate?
- Are word limits and formatting requirements satisfied?

Revise only where the review identifies a meaningful improvement. Do not rewrite correct prose merely to introduce superficial variation.

## 16. Final acceptance criteria

An answer is ready for submission when it:

- Fully addresses the question and relevant rubric criteria.
- Demonstrates subject-specific understanding and appropriate critical reasoning.
- Uses evidence accurately and transparently.
- Expresses ideas clearly, directly and with appropriate nuance.
- Avoids repetitive phrasing, generic filler and unnecessary verbosity.
- Uses a structure suited to the task rather than a universal response template.
- Maintains consistency with the wider assignment.
- Meets academic conventions, referencing requirements and word limits.

**Governing principle:** Write to communicate the reasoning, not to display academic-sounding language. Be specific where the evidence is specific, cautious where the evidence is limited, direct where the conclusion is clear, and detailed where the question demands it.

## 17. Repository Mechanics (Citations, Figures, Tables, Code, and Spelling)

### Citations (pandoc syntax)

When `referencing.style` uses BibTeX/CSL (rather than an internal quote scheme such as `P1`–`P20`):

| Need | Write |
|---|---|
| Parenthetical | `[@smith2021]` → (Smith 2021) |
| With page | `[@smith2021, p. 4]` |
| Several | `[@smith2021; @lee2020]` |
| Narrative | `@smith2021 argues…` → Smith (2021) argues… |
| Suppress author | `[-@smith2021]` |

The reference list is generated at export from `.bib` and the CSL style. Never type reference lists by hand. When `referencing.style` is `"n/a"` with an internal scheme (described in `referencing.notes`), follow that scheme exactly.

### Figures, tables, code

- **Figure:** `![Smoothed series vs raw (window = 5).](../work/results/figures/Q2-smoothing.png){#fig:q2-smooth width=80%}` and refer to it in the text ("Figure 1 shows…").
- **Table:** paste the Markdown table from `work/results/tables/…` with a `Table: caption` line, but only if it came from a logged run. Never retype numbers.
- **Code:** reference `work/code/src/file.py` (function names, line ranges). Include a short excerpt (≤15 lines) only when the rubric rewards discussing implementation choices.

### Australian English (`spelling: en-AU`)

Unless `context/assignment.md` specifies another locale, use Australian English:
`-ise`/`-yse` (organise, analyse), `-our` (behaviour), `-re` (centre), `-ll-` (modelling, labelled), `licence` (noun) / `license` (verb), `practice` (noun) / `practise` (verb), `program` (computing). Format dates as `19 October 2026`.
