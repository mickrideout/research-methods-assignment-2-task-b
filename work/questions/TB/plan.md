## Reasoning outline

- **Central position / design recommendation:** a randomised two-group online experiment can isolate whether the *pessimistic framing* typical of housing content on social media, as distinct from the housing facts themselves, lowers young non-homeowning Australians' perceived likelihood of owning a home. The design is feasible within AUD 1,000 and 100 hours. It is tested with Welch's t-test, a Week 9 test that matches two independent groups and a continuous outcome. Its main threats are ecological validity, sample representativeness and short-term measurement, and each has a matching future study.
- **PICO** (add-info #line.76–86):
  - **P:** Australians aged 18–30 who do not own a home.
  - **I:** a simulated feed of 8 housing posts written in pessimistic "crisis" framing.
  - **C:** the same 8 facts in neutral framing.
  - **O:** perceived likelihood of owning a home within ten years, on a 0–100 slider.
- **Logical line of reasoning:**
  1. **Claim 1 (headings 1–2): the hypothesis isolates one social-media ingredient and is testable.**
     - **Evidence:** PICO guidance and the significance trap (add-info #line.86, #line.91); the testability requirement (lecture #line.134); H0 as no difference (lecture #line.141).
     - **Reasoning:** holding the facts constant means any group difference can only come from framing. This answers the topic's "how does social media influence" with a mechanism rather than an exposure dose. It also fixes the weakness the judge found in gpt-proposal-11, where tone and content were bundled.
     - **Draft H1 (≈45 words):** "Australians aged 18–30 who do not own a home and view a simulated social media feed of pessimistically framed housing posts will report a lower perceived likelihood of owning a home within ten years than those viewing the same housing facts in neutral framing."
     - **Draft H0 (≈40 words):** "Perceived likelihood of owning a home within ten years will not differ between Australians aged 18–30 without a home who view pessimistically framed housing posts and those who view the same facts in neutral framing."
  2. **Claim 2 (heading 3): the variables follow directly from H1, and the confounders are plausible influences on both housing outlook and social-media habits.**
     - **IV:** post framing (pessimistic or neutral), randomised.
     - **DV:** perceived ten-year ownership likelihood (0–100).
     - **Confounders (≥3):**
       - household income or savings
       - living arrangement and location (renting or living with parents; capital city or regional housing market)
       - expected family financial help
       - prior weekly housing-content use or baseline housing pessimism
     - **Control:** all are measured before exposure and balanced by randomisation (lecture #line.175–176; add-info #line.35).
  3. **Claim 3 (heading 4): the plan is practical and fully budgeted.**
     - **Sampling:** voluntary-response convenience sampling via paid Instagram/TikTok ads, university notices and community groups, screened for age 18–30, Australian residence and not owning a home. Why: an online, low-cost route to a mostly online population. Report which method, why, and the generalisability cost (lecture #line.104, #line.119–122).
     - **Sample:** target about 120 completers, 60 per group, provisional until the power run (D5). If the run needs more per group (e.g. about 64), raise the target and rebalance the budget, for example incentives AUD 5 × 130 = 650, ads 250, contingency 100.
     - **Location and equipment:** online, Australia-wide; a survey platform with a built-in randomiser; mock posts made in a design tool; participants' own devices.
     - **Process:** screen, then consent, then confounder questions, then random allocation, then view the 8 posts, then the DV, then a tone manipulation check, then an attention check, then a debrief with balanced housing information.
     - **Budget (planned allocations, not quoted prices):** incentives AUD 5 × 120 = 600; ads 250; contingency or pilot 150. Total 1,000.
     - **Hours:** stimuli and pilot 25; ethics and setup 15; recruitment and monitoring 25; analysis 15; write-up 20. Total 100.
  4. **Claim 4 (heading 5): Welch's t-test is the right primary test.**
     - **Why it fits:** 2 independent groups, a between-subjects design and a continuous DV (lecture #line.191, #line.195). Welch's is used because equal variances cannot be assumed, and variance equality is what decides Student's versus Welch's (lecture #line.195). Staff accepted this exact choice (add-info #line.10, #line.68).
     - **Assumptions:**
       - independence: one response per person and random allocation
       - approximately normal DV within groups, which a 0–100 slider may violate at the floor or ceiling, so Mann–Whitney U is the fallback (lecture #line.195)
       - continuous measurement level
     - **Direction rationale (D2, one clause, about 15 words):** e.g. "H1 is directional because barrier-focused, emotive framing should make identical facts seem less surmountable."
     - **Interpretation (D3, two-sided):** α = .05, two-sided, so an unexpected opposite effect would also be detected. Say this in one clause. It reconciles the directional H1 with the two-sided test. Reject H0 if p < .05, then check the sign: support for H1 needs the neutral-framing mean to be higher. Report the mean difference, its 95% CI and Cohen's d (rubric #line.9 HD: "how results would be interpreted").
     - **Power (D5):** state the sample size per group from the logged power run only. Do not write a figure until it is in the manifest.
     - Leave out the ANCOVA sensitivity check. The direction and two-sided clauses use up the words it would need.
  5. **Claim 5 (heading 6): three design-specific limitations, each matched to a future study.**
     - (a) A brief, researcher-made feed lacks algorithmic personalisation and repeated exposure, so the effect size may not transfer to real feeds. **Future:** a multi-day field exposure, for example a browser-extension feed.
     - (b) A self-selected online sample over-represents engaged, digitally active young people, which limits generalisation (lecture #line.104, #line.107, #line.122). **Future:** a stratified quota sample by state and income (lecture #line.96).
     - (c) An immediate, single-item, self-reported outcome may capture passing mood rather than a lasting perception, and may be prone to demand effects. **Future:** a delayed follow-up after 1–2 weeks with a multi-item measure.
     - Each limitation states its impact on the findings (rubric #line.10 HD; FAQ #line.79).
  6. **Claim 6 (heading 7): a rival conclusion is producible by named manipulations.**
     - **Rival narrative:** "Young Australians' housing pessimism reflects their finances, not what they see on social media."
     - **How it would be produced:**
       - recruit only low-income renters in Sydney and Melbourne, whose ratings sit near the floor (selection bias, lecture #line.205)
       - measure the DV a week after exposure, once the effect has faded
       - report only the unadjusted Mann–Whitney result and omit the effect size (cherry-picking or reporting bias, lecture #line.206)
     - The result would be "no difference between groups", presented as evidence that finances drive housing perceptions.
     - This refers to specific earlier choices (sample, timing, test) and names the changes (FAQ #line.75). It is a substantive rival claim, not just a null result (FAQ #line.77).
     - **Decided (D4):** use this narrative only.
