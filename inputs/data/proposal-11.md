## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, randomised exposure to a positive homeownership success-story video compilation will cause higher optimism about one's own future homeownership than exposure to a negative housing-crisis compilation of equal length.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

There is no difference in post-exposure homeownership optimism between Australians aged 18–30 randomly assigned to view a positive success-story compilation versus a negative housing-crisis compilation.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** narrative valence condition (positive vs negative, randomly assigned). **DV:** homeownership optimism (post-exposure 6-item scale). **Covariates:** baseline optimism, current income, and prior content exposure.

## 4. Data Collection

Randomised online experiment with 90 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university lists and AUD $180 of social-media advertising, randomly assigned via Qualtrics to a five-minute positive or negative housing-content compilation drawn from publicly available, creator-permitted videos. Baseline optimism is measured pre-exposure; post-exposure optimism is measured immediately after. Participants receive a AUD $9 e-voucher each (AUD $810), keeping total cost within budget. Informed consent, debriefing explaining the manipulation, and voluntary withdrawal are provided.

## 5. Statistical Tests

An independent-samples t-test compares post-exposure optimism between the positive and negative conditions, matching a two-group randomised predictor with a continuous, approximately normal outcome. Baseline optimism, income, and prior exposure are checked for balance and entered as covariates in a supplementary ANCOVA. Assumptions include independence, normality of residuals within groups (Shapiro-Wilk), and homogeneity of variance (Levene's test); Welch's t-test is reported if variances differ. Significance is set at α = .05, with a 95% confidence interval for the mean difference and Cohen's d as the effect size. Random assignment supports a causal interpretation of any observed difference in optimism.

## 6. Limitations

A single brief exposure may not generalise to cumulative everyday content mixes (Vogel et al., 2014). Convenience sampling limits generalisability beyond socially engaged participants. Demand characteristics may inflate self-reported optimism post-manipulation. Only one operationalisation of narrative valence is tested. A future field study varying exposure duration and content source would strengthen ecological validity.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant difference could be overstated as proof negative content permanently lowers optimism, by ignoring the transient exposure and omitting baseline optimism as a covariate. Reporting only the largest pre-post changes would inflate the effect—a risk of selective analysis, not sound practice.
