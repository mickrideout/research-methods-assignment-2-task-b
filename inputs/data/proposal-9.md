## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, greater frequency of upward social comparison to peers' housing situations on Instagram will be positively associated with higher housing-related anxiety.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

There is no association between frequency of upward social comparison to peers' housing situations on Instagram and housing-related anxiety among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** upward housing-related social-comparison frequency (5-item scale, continuous). **DV:** housing-related anxiety (adapted GAD-7, continuous). **Confounders:** trait social-comparison orientation, household income, and daily Instagram use.

## 4. Data Collection

Cross-sectional online survey of 120 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university networks and AUD $150 of targeted Instagram advertising. A 10-minute survey administers an upward-comparison frequency scale adapted from social-comparison research (Festinger, 1954; Vogel et al., 2014), the housing-adapted GAD-7 (Spitzer et al., 2006), a brief comparison-orientation measure, income, and daily-use minutes. Participants enter a draw for five AUD $80 vouchers (AUD $400), keeping total cost near AUD $550. Online consent, voluntary participation, and anonymised handling apply throughout.

## 5. Statistical Tests

Simple linear regression tests whether upward-comparison frequency predicts housing-related anxiety, appropriate for one continuous predictor and one continuous, approximately normal outcome; Pearson's r is reported alongside the regression as a familiar correlation index. Comparison orientation, income, and daily use are added as covariates in a multiple regression model. Assumptions include linearity, independence of residuals, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, and absence of high-leverage outliers. If assumptions are violated, a bootstrapped regression with bias-corrected confidence intervals is used instead. Significance is set at α = .05, with 95% confidence intervals and R² as the effect size. As the design is observational, results describe an association, not a causal comparison effect.

## 6. Limitations

Self-selected, Instagram-using samples limit generalisability to non-users. Both measures rely on self-report, risking common-method variance. The cross-sectional design cannot establish whether comparison precedes anxiety or vice versa. Trait comparison orientation may only partly capture stable individual differences, leaving residual confounding. A future experience-sampling study measuring comparisons and anxiety in daily life would strengthen temporal inference.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant association could be overstated as evidence comparison 'triggers' anxiety by omitting trait comparison-orientation as a confounder or emphasising significance over a modest R². Reporting results only for frequent Instagram users would inflate the apparent relationship. This is a risk of selective analysis, not recommended practice.
