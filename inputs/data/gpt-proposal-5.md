## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among Australian non-homeowners aged 18–30, more frequent perceived encounters with false or misleading housing claims on social media will be associated with lower trust in Australian housing-policy information.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

Among Australian non-homeowners aged 18–30, perceived frequency of encountering false or misleading housing claims on social media will not be associated with trust in Australian housing-policy information.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**Predictor:** perceived misinformation-encounter frequency, five ordered categories. **Outcome:** trust in housing-policy information, mean of four 1–7 items. **Confounders:** political ideology, education, news literacy, general institutional trust, and weekly housing-content exposure.

## 4. Data Collection

A cross-sectional online survey will recruit 150 Australian residents aged 18–30 who own no home and use social media weekly. University lists, housing groups, and AUD 200 advertising will support recruitment. Respondents rate encounter frequency, policy-information trust, news literacy, and confounders; AHURI documents relevant aspiration constraints (Stone et al., 2020). Examples define misinformation without asking participants to supply posts; false news can diffuse unusually widely online (Vosoughi et al., 2018). Ten AUD 40 voucher draws produce about AUD 600 cost and 70 hours. Consent, anonymity, optional questions, secure storage, and no handles or private-content collection apply.

## 5. Statistical Tests

Spearman’s rank correlation is the primary test because exposure is ordinal, trust is a bounded composite score, and a monotonic association is hypothesised without assuming equal exposure intervals or bivariate normality. A scatterplot of ranked values will assess monotonicity; independent observations and influential cases will also be checked. Spearman’s ρ, its 95% bootstrap confidence interval, and a two-sided α = .05 test will be reported. A supplementary multiple linear regression will enter indicator-coded exposure categories and adjust for ideology, education, news literacy, institutional trust, and total exposure, with residual diagnostics and robust standard errors if needed. Results will be described as association: perceived misinformation may reduce trust, but distrust may also increase perceived misinformation recognition.

## 6. Limitations

Participants cannot reliably verify every claim, so the predictor measures perceived rather than objectively coded misinformation. General distrust may influence both exposure reports and policy trust despite adjustment. News-literacy self-report can overstate skill, while convenience recruitment may attract politically attentive users. Cross-sectional timing prevents temporal or causal conclusions. A future randomised experiment could expose participants to pretested accurate versus inaccurate housing claims, include corrections and manipulation checks, and measure immediate trust while ensuring debriefing corrects every false statement.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

An analyst could omit institutional trust, collapse exposure categories after inspecting results, or describe correlation as proof misinformation destroys trust. Alternatively selecting only politically disaffected respondents could reverse emphasis. These flexible choices would create narratives rather than robust evidence.
