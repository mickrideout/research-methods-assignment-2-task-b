## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Within non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, days with above-personal-average housing-content social media exposure will show lower same-day perceived housing affordability than days with below-average exposure, within the same individuals.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

Within non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, there is no difference in same-day perceived housing affordability between above-average and below-average housing-content exposure days for the same individuals.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** daily housing-content exposure level (above vs below personal 14-day average, within-person). **DV:** same-day perceived affordability (single-item, 0–100). **Confounders:** day of week, concurrent news events, and daily mood.

## 4. Data Collection

Short longitudinal diary study with 20 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university networks and housing-forum posts (no paid advertising required). Participants complete a 1-minute nightly mobile survey for 14 consecutive days recording housing-content exposure minutes, affordability perception, and mood, via a free survey-platform link. Participants receive a AUD $40 voucher on completion (AUD $800 total), keeping costs well within budget. Consent covers repeated contact, voluntary withdrawal at any point, and anonymised, aggregated storage of daily responses.

## 5. Statistical Tests

A paired-samples t-test compares each participant's mean affordability rating on above-average versus below-average exposure days, appropriate for within-person, repeated-measures comparison of one continuous outcome across two matched conditions. Day of week and concurrent news events are noted as covariates for a supplementary multilevel model if time permits. Assumptions include normality of the paired differences (Shapiro-Wilk) and independence between participants (though not within-person observations). If normality is violated, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test is used instead. Significance is set at α = .05, with a 95% confidence interval for the mean difference and Cohen's dz as the effect size. The within-subject design strengthens, but does not guarantee, causal interpretation given uncontrolled daily events.

## 6. Limitations

The small, self-selected sample limits statistical power and generalisability. Daily self-report is burdensome and may cause attrition or reactivity to monitoring. Fourteen days cannot capture longer housing-market cycles (Australian Housing and Urban Research Institute, 2020). Concurrent news events are only partially controlled. A future study could extend the diary period and include an objective screen-time app to reduce recall bias.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant difference could be overstated as proof exposure 'causes' daily affordability shifts by ignoring concurrent news events and daily mood as confounders. Excluding participants with high attrition could bias the sample toward highly engaged users, inflating the effect. This is a risk of selective analysis, not recommended practice.
