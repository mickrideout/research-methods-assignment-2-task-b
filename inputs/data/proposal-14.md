## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, those with high trust in social-media housing information will be more likely to view the housing system as unfair toward their generation than those with low trust.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

There is no association between level of trust in social-media housing information (high/low) and whether non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30 view the housing system as fair or unfair toward their generation.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** trust in social-media housing information (high/low, median split). **DV:** perceived system fairness (fair/unfair, binary). **Confounders:** political ideology, household income, and parental home-ownership.

## 4. Data Collection

Cross-sectional online survey of 140 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university networks, community forums, and AUD $160 of advertising. A 9-minute survey administers a validated-style social-media trust scale (median-split into high/low), a binary fairness-perception item, political ideology, income, and parental home-ownership. Participants enter a draw for six AUD $70 vouchers (AUD $420), keeping total cost near AUD $580. Informed online consent, voluntary participation, and anonymised storage meet standard ethics requirements.

## 5. Statistical Tests

A chi-square test of independence examines the association between trust group (high/low) and fairness perception (fair/unfair), appropriate for two categorical variables. Political ideology, income, and parental home-ownership are examined via a stratified analysis and a supplementary logistic regression to adjust for confounding. Assumptions include independence of observations and expected cell counts of at least five in each of the four cells. If expected counts fall below five, Fisher's exact test is used instead. Significance is set at α = .05, with Cramér's V reported as the effect size and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals from the logistic model. As groups are observational, findings describe an association, not a causal trust effect.

## 6. Limitations

Median-split dichotomisation of trust discards information and can reduce statistical power (Australian Housing and Urban Research Institute, 2015). Non-probability sampling limits generalisability. Cross-sectional timing cannot establish whether trust precedes fairness perceptions. Political ideology may confound both variables despite stratification. A future study using continuous trust scores and structural equation modelling could better model the relationship.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant chi-square could be reframed as proof social media 'creates' perceptions of unfairness by ignoring the ideology confound or reporting only the high-trust subgroup. Choosing an extreme trust threshold instead of a median split would further inflate the association—a risk of selective analysis.
