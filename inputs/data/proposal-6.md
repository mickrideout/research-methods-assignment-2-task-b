## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, higher frequency of actively posting or commenting about housing issues on social media will predict greater odds of having contacted a representative or signed a housing-related petition in the past six months.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

Frequency of actively posting or commenting about housing issues on social media does not predict the odds of having contacted a representative or signed a housing-related petition among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** frequency of active housing-related posting/commenting (weekly count, continuous). **DV:** contacted a representative or signed a petition in six months (yes/no). **Confounders:** political interest, age, and education level.

## 4. Data Collection

Cross-sectional online survey of 140 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university networks, housing-advocacy Facebook/Reddit groups, and AUD $150 of advertising. A 9-minute survey records weekly active-posting frequency, binary political-action history, political interest, age, and education. Participants enter a draw for six AUD $70 vouchers (AUD $420), keeping total cost near AUD $570. Ethics approval, informed online consent, voluntary participation, and anonymised storage apply; no public posts are collected or linked to identities.

## 5. Statistical Tests

Binary logistic regression predicts the odds of political action (yes/no) from active-posting frequency, appropriate because the outcome is binary and the predictor is continuous. Political interest, age, and education are entered as covariates to adjust for confounding. Assumptions include independence of observations, linearity between the predictor and the log-odds of the outcome (Box-Tidwell test), absence of multicollinearity (VIF), and adequately sized outcome groups. If linearity in the logit is violated, posting frequency will be categorised into tertiles as a fallback. Significance is set at α = .05, with odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and Nagelkerke R² reported as effect-size measures. The observational design supports association, not causal, claims about posting behaviour.

## 6. Limitations

Self-selected, socially engaged samples likely over-represent politically active young adults, limiting generalisability. Self-reported action history is subject to recall and social-desirability bias. Cross-sectional data cannot establish whether posting precedes or follows action. Residual confounding from pre-existing activism traits may persist despite covariates (Australian Housing and Urban Research Institute, 2015). A future panel study tracking posting and action over time would clarify sequence.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant odds ratio could be reframed as proof posting 'drives' activism by omitting political interest as a confounder, or emphasising significance over a modest odds ratio near 1. Excluding low-frequency posters from the model would inflate the effect—a risk of selective analysis, not recommended practice.
