## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, the negative association between housing-content social media exposure and trust in housing policy will be stronger for participants with more left-leaning political ideology than for those more right-leaning.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

Political ideology does not moderate the association between housing-content social media exposure and trust in housing policy among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30; the interaction coefficient does not differ from zero.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** housing-content exposure (weekly minutes) and political ideology (continuous left-right scale). **DV:** trust in housing policy (continuous scale). **Confounders:** age, income, and general institutional trust.

## 4. Data Collection

Cross-sectional online survey of 150 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university networks, varied political-leaning community groups, and AUD $180 of advertising. A 10-minute survey records weekly housing-content exposure, a single-item ideology scale, policy-trust scale, age, income, and general institutional trust. Participants enter a draw for six AUD $75 vouchers (AUD $450), keeping total cost near AUD $630. Informed online consent, voluntary participation, and anonymised storage meet standard ethics requirements.

## 5. Statistical Tests

A moderated multiple regression tests whether the exposure–trust association is conditional on ideology, entering exposure, ideology, and their mean-centred interaction term, appropriate for continuous predictors, a continuous moderator, and a continuous outcome. Age, income, and institutional trust are included as covariates. Assumptions include linearity, independence of residuals, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, and absence of multicollinearity (VIF), checked after mean-centring to reduce interaction-term collinearity. If assumptions are violated, a robust standard-error regression is used instead. Significance is set at α = .05, with 95% confidence intervals for the interaction coefficient and simple-slopes analysis at ±1 SD of ideology as the effect-size illustration. Given the observational design, results describe conditional associations, not causal moderation.

## 6. Limitations

A single-item ideology measure may lack precision compared to validated multi-item scales. Non-probability sampling limits generalisability across the political spectrum (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2021). Cross-sectional timing prevents causal inference. Residual confounding from prior housing experience may persist. A future study using a validated ideology scale and longitudinal data would strengthen the moderation test.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant interaction could be reframed as proof ideology 'determines' susceptibility to content by reporting only the simple slope for one ideology group. Choosing an extreme ideology split instead of continuous modelling would exaggerate the pattern—a risk of selective analysis.
