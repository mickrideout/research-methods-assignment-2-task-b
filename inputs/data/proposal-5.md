## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, more frequent self-reported encounters with housing-related misinformation on social media will be associated with lower trust in government housing policy, measured on an ordinal trust scale.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

There is no association between frequency of encountering housing-related misinformation on social media and trust in government housing policy among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** self-reported frequency of encountering housing misinformation (5-point ordinal). **DV:** trust in government housing policy (5-point ordinal). **Confounders:** political ideology, education level, and general institutional trust.

## 4. Data Collection

Cross-sectional online survey of 130 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university mailing lists, community Facebook groups, and AUD $150 of targeted advertising. A 10-minute Qualtrics survey measures perceived misinformation frequency (with example prompts, not verification of specific claims), policy trust, political ideology, education, and general institutional trust. Participants enter a draw for five AUD $80 vouchers (AUD $400), keeping total cost near AUD $550. Consent is obtained online, participation is anonymous and voluntary, and no false content is created or shown to participants.

## 5. Statistical Tests

A Spearman rank-order correlation tests the association between misinformation-encounter frequency and policy trust, appropriate given both variables are ordinal and may not satisfy interval-level or normality assumptions required for Pearson's r. Political ideology, education, and institutional trust are examined via partial Spearman correlation to check whether the association persists after adjustment. Assumptions include independence of observations and a monotonic (not necessarily linear) relationship, checked via scatterplot. Significance is set at α = .05, with a 95% bootstrapped confidence interval for rho reported as the effect size. As this is an observational design based on perceived exposure, results are interpreted as an association, not a causal effect of misinformation.

## 6. Limitations

Self-reported misinformation exposure is subjective and cannot verify actual falsity of content viewed (Vosoughi et al., 2018). Non-probability sampling limits generalisability. Cross-sectional timing prevents establishing temporal precedence. Political ideology may confound both misinformation perception and policy trust despite adjustment. A future study could pair self-report with verified platform exposure logs and a longitudinal design to clarify direction.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant negative correlation could be reframed as proof misinformation 'erodes' trust by omitting the ideology covariate or emphasising a significant p-value despite a weak rho. Restricting analysis to participants reporting frequent exposure only would inflate the relationship—a risk of selective analysis, not recommended practice.
