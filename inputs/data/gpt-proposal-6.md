## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among Australian non-homeowners aged 18–30, more frequent active posting or commenting about housing on social media will be associated with higher odds of undertaking at least one housing-policy action during the following month.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

Among Australian non-homeowners aged 18–30, active housing-related posting or commenting frequency will not predict the odds of undertaking a housing-policy action during the following month.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**Predictor:** baseline weekly housing-post/comment count. **Outcome:** any verified-by-self-report policy action by follow-up, yes/no. **Confounders:** prior political participation, internal political efficacy, education, political interest, and total social-media use.

## 4. Data Collection

A two-wave online survey will recruit 150 Australian residents aged 18–30 who own no home. University, renter, and youth-organisation notices plus AUD 150 advertising recruit participants. Baseline records active engagement and confounders; 30 days later, participants report whether they contacted a representative, signed a petition, attended a meeting, or made a submission. Social-media participation is often positively associated with civic participation (Boulianne, 2015). Fifteen AUD 40 completion vouchers cost AUD 600; total cost is about AUD 800 and workload 85 hours. Consent, coded linkage, separate contact details, and deletion after payment protect privacy.

## 5. Statistical Tests

Binary logistic regression is appropriate because the outcome is dichotomous and the focal predictor is a count. The exponentiated posting coefficient estimates the change in odds of action per additional weekly engagement, first unadjusted and then adjusted for prior participation, efficacy, education, political interest, and total use. Efficacy uses Niemi et al.’s (1991) scale. Linearity of the continuous logit, independent observations, multicollinearity, influential cases, and adequate events per parameter will be checked; posting count may be log-transformed if strongly skewed. If separation occurs, categories will be combined according to a prespecified rule and results treated cautiously. At α = .05, adjusted odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and Nagelkerke R² will be reported. The lag improves temporal ordering but does not justify causal language because posting is not randomised.

## 6. Limitations

Policy action is self-reported and combines behaviours requiring different effort. Attrition could selectively remove less engaged participants, while recruiting through advocacy groups may inflate both posting and action. The one-month window misses later activity. Prior political motivation and network ties may remain confounders despite baseline adjustment, so temporal precedence alone does not prove causation. A future study could recruit a probability-based panel, retain separate action outcomes, and randomise prompts encouraging active discussion to estimate whether engagement changes subsequent behaviour.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

Omitting prior participation, counting likes as substantive action, or analysing only follow-up completers without attrition checks could make posting appear transformative. Presenting an odds ratio as a risk difference would also exaggerate magnitude. These choices exemplify selective analysis.
