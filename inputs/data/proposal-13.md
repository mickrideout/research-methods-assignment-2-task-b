## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, more frequent exposure to property-investment "finfluencer" content on social media will be positively associated with self-reported intention to increase deposit savings over the next twelve months.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

There is no association between frequency of exposure to property-investment finfluencer content and self-reported intention to increase deposit savings among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** weekly exposure to property-finfluencer content (self-report, continuous). **DV:** savings-increase intention (7-item continuous scale). **Confounders:** current income, existing financial literacy, and parental financial support.

## 4. Data Collection

Cross-sectional online survey of 120 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university networks, personal-finance Facebook/Reddit communities, and AUD $150 of advertising. A 10-minute survey measures weekly finfluencer exposure minutes, savings intention, income, a brief financial-literacy quiz, and parental financial support. Participants enter a draw for five AUD $80 vouchers (AUD $400), keeping total cost near AUD $550. Consent is obtained online, participation is voluntary and anonymous, and no specific creators or accounts are identified.

## 5. Statistical Tests

Simple linear regression tests whether finfluencer exposure predicts savings intention, appropriate for one continuous predictor and one continuous, approximately normal outcome. Income, financial literacy, and parental support are added as covariates in a multiple regression model to adjust for confounding. Assumptions include linearity, independence of residuals, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals (Q–Q plots), and absence of high-leverage outliers (Cook's distance). If assumptions are violated, a bootstrapped regression with bias-corrected confidence intervals is used instead. Significance is set at α = .05, with 95% confidence intervals and R² reported as the effect size. As the design is cross-sectional, results describe an association, not a causal financial effect.

## 6. Limitations

Self-reported intention may not translate into actual saving behaviour. Non-probability sampling from finance-interested communities limits generalisability. Finfluencer content quality and accuracy are not verified, risking exposure to misinformation (Vosoughi et al., 2018). Residual confounding from risk tolerance may persist despite covariates. A future study tracking actual bank-verified savings over time would strengthen validity.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant positive association could be overstated as proof finfluencers 'boost' saving by omitting financial literacy as a confounder or emphasising significance over a small R². Reporting only high-exposure participants would inflate the relationship—a risk of selective analysis, not sound practice.
