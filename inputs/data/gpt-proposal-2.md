## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among Australian non-homeowners aged 18–30 who consume housing-crisis content, TikTok-primary viewers will report higher housing-worry scores than YouTube-primary viewers.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

Among Australian non-homeowners aged 18–30 who consume housing-crisis content, mean housing-worry scores will not differ between TikTok-primary and YouTube-primary viewers.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**Independent variable:** primary platform for housing-crisis content, TikTok or YouTube. **Dependent variable:** mean housing-worry score from seven purpose-written items. **Confounders:** total social-media time, general anxiety, income, housing-cost burden, and metropolitan residence.

## 4. Data Collection

A cross-sectional online survey will recruit 140 Australian residents aged 18–30 who own no home, view housing content weekly, and primarily use TikTok or YouTube for it. Recruitment uses university lists, youth groups, and AUD 200 advertisements. The survey measures platform, housing worry, unmodified GAD-7 general anxiety (Spitzer et al., 2006), and confounders; AHURI evidence informs housing wording (Stone et al., 2020). Eight AUD 50 voucher draws produce an estimated AUD 600 cost and 70 researcher hours. Consent, distress resources, anonymity, withdrawal before submission, and no usernames or private-content access are required.

## 5. Statistical Tests

An independent-samples t-test will compare mean housing-worry scores across two mutually exclusive, independently sampled platform groups. This matches a binary categorical predictor and approximately continuous outcome. Independence follows from one response per participant; histograms and Q–Q plots will assess within-group normality, and Levene’s test will assess equal variances. Welch’s t-test will replace the pooled test if variances differ; a Mann–Whitney U test will be a sensitivity analysis for severe skew or influential outliers. A supplementary multiple regression will adjust for total use, GAD-7 score, income, housing-cost burden, and residence. With α = .05, results will include the mean difference, 95% confidence interval, and Hedges’ g. Because participants select platforms, findings concern association rather than causal platform effects.

## 6. Limitations

Platform groups may differ before exposure, including in age, anxiety, interests, and algorithmic history. Self-reported primary platform and content use are vulnerable to recall error. Purpose-written housing-worry items may not capture clinical anxiety, whereas GAD-7 was validated for general anxiety (Spitzer et al., 2006). Recruitment excludes people who avoid housing content, and cross-sectional timing prevents causal inference. A future experiment could randomise matched TikTok-style and YouTube-style presentations while holding claims, duration, and audiovisual quality constant, then measure immediate state worry.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

Selecting only heavy users, omitting general anxiety, or relabelling an adjusted association as a causal TikTok effect could support an alarmist narrative. Reporting only the p-value while hiding overlapping distributions would further exaggerate practical importance; these are selective practices.
