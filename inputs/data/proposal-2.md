## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30 who primarily consume housing-crisis content on TikTok will report significantly higher housing-related anxiety, measured with a housing-adapted GAD-7, than those who primarily consume equivalent content on YouTube.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

There is no difference in mean housing-related anxiety scores between non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30 who primarily consume housing-crisis content on TikTok versus YouTube; any observed mean difference is attributable to chance.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** primary platform for housing-crisis content (TikTok vs YouTube, categorical). **DV:** housing-related anxiety (adapted GAD-7, continuous). **Confounders:** daily total social-media use, baseline trait anxiety, and household income.

## 4. Data Collection

Cross-sectional online survey of 120 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30 (60 primarily-TikTok, 60 primarily-YouTube housing-content viewers), recruited via university networks, Facebook housing groups, and AUD $200 of targeted advertising. An 8-minute survey measures platform preference, a 7-item GAD-7 adapted to housing worries (Spitzer et al., 2006), daily social-media minutes, a brief trait-anxiety screen, and income. Participants enter a draw for eight AUD $50 vouchers (AUD $400), keeping costs under AUD $650. Ethics approval, informed consent, and anonymous data handling apply throughout.

## 5. Statistical Tests

An independent-samples t-test compares mean housing-anxiety scores between TikTok-primary and YouTube-primary groups, matching one categorical predictor with two independent groups against one continuous, approximately normal outcome. Social-media minutes, trait anxiety, and income are examined as covariates in a supplementary ANCOVA. Assumptions include independence of observations, approximate normality within groups (Shapiro-Wilk), and homogeneity of variance (Levene's test); if violated, Welch's t-test is reported instead. Significance is set at α = .05, with a 95% confidence interval for the mean difference and Cohen's d as the effect size. Because platform use is self-selected, not randomised, results describe an association, not a causal platform effect.

## 6. Limitations

Self-selected platform groups introduce selection bias; pre-existing anxiety may drive platform choice rather than the reverse. Self-reported anxiety is subject to recall and social-desirability bias. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and unmet housing aspirations independently affect wellbeing (Australian Housing and Urban Research Institute, 2020). A future randomised experiment exposing participants to matched content could isolate platform-specific causal effects.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant mean difference could be reframed as proof TikTok 'causes' anxiety by omitting the self-selection confound and trait-anxiety covariate. Excluding low-engagement YouTube users, or emphasising the p-value over the modest effect size, would overstate practical importance. This illustrates a risk of selective analysis, not sound practice.
