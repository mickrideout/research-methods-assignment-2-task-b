## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among Australians aged 18–30 who do not own a home, more weekly time spent passively viewing property-listing and real-estate content on Instagram is associated with lower perceived housing affordability, measured via a validated affordability-perception scale.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

There is no association between weekly minutes spent passively viewing property-listing content on Instagram and perceived housing affordability among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30; any observed correlation reflects sampling variability rather than a true population relationship.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** weekly minutes passively viewing property/real-estate content on Instagram (self-report, continuous). **DV:** perceived housing affordability (7-point scale). **Confounders:** household income, current employment status, and parental home-ownership status.

## 4. Data Collection

Cross-sectional online survey of 110 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university mailing lists, Reddit housing forums, and AUD $150 of targeted Instagram/Facebook advertising. A 10-minute Qualtrics survey measures weekly Instagram property-content viewing minutes, a 7-item affordability-perception scale, income, employment status, and parental home-ownership. Participants enter a draw for five AUD $100 gift cards (AUD $500), keeping total cost near AUD $650. Consent is obtained online, participation is voluntary and anonymous, and no platform content is scraped without authorisation.

## 5. Statistical Tests

Simple linear regression tests whether weekly Instagram property-content viewing time predicts perceived housing affordability, matching one continuous predictor with one continuous, approximately interval-level outcome. Household income, employment status, and parental home-ownership are added as covariates in a multiple regression model to adjust for confounding. Assumptions checked include linearity, independence of residuals, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals (Q–Q plots), and absence of high-leverage outliers (Cook's distance). If normality or homoscedasticity is violated, a bootstrapped regression with bias-corrected confidence intervals will be used instead. Significance is set at α = .05, with 95% confidence intervals and R² reported as the effect size. Because the design is cross-sectional and non-experimental, results are interpreted as associations, not causal effects.

## 6. Limitations

Non-probability, self-selected sampling limits generalisability beyond socially engaged, Instagram-using young adults (Parkinson et al., 2019). Self-reported viewing time is subject to recall bias and may not reflect platform-recorded usage. The cross-sectional design prevents causal or temporal inference. Residual confounding (e.g., pre-existing financial anxiety) may remain despite covariate adjustment, and the modest sample limits power for small effects. A future study could use platform-verified screen-time data and a longitudinal design to strengthen temporal and measurement validity.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

Findings could be reframed causally—claiming viewing 'creates' pessimism—by ignoring the correlational design, omitting income as a confounder, or emphasising statistical significance over the small effect size. Restricting analysis to high-viewing participants only would inflate the apparent relationship. This reflects a risk of selective analysis, not recommended practice.
