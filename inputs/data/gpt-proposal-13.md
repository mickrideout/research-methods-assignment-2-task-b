## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among Australian non-homeowners aged 18–30, more frequent exposure to property-finfluencer content will be associated with greater willingness to use high-risk investments to build a home deposit.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

Among Australian non-homeowners aged 18–30, property-finfluencer exposure frequency will not be associated with willingness to use high-risk investments to build a home deposit.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**Predictor:** weekly property-finfluencer exposure frequency. **Outcome:** mean willingness across four high-risk deposit strategies. **Confounders:** financial literacy, disposable income, existing investments, general risk tolerance, parental assistance, and intended purchase horizon.

## 4. Data Collection

A cross-sectional online survey will recruit 160 Australian residents aged 18–30 who own no home and use social media weekly. University, youth-finance, and renter channels plus AUD 220 advertising recruit participants. The survey defines finfluencer content neutrally, measures exposure, uses hypothetical willingness items, and records confounders. Housing aspirations vary with financial constraints (Stone et al., 2020); misinformation concerns motivate careful wording (Vosoughi et al., 2018). Twelve AUD 40 voucher draws make costs about AUD 700 and workload 75 hours. Consent, financial-risk disclaimers, anonymity, optional asset questions, secure storage, and no account or transaction access protect participants.

## 5. Statistical Tests

Multiple linear regression will test whether continuous finfluencer exposure predicts the approximately interval willingness composite after adjustment for financial literacy, disposable income, existing investments, risk tolerance, parental assistance, and purchase horizon. This model fits one continuous focal predictor, one continuous outcome, and several plausible confounders. Diagnostics will examine linearity, independent observations, normal and homoscedastic residuals, multicollinearity, and influential cases. If willingness has a floor effect, ordinal logistic regression on a prespecified low/moderate/high outcome will be a sensitivity analysis, provided proportional odds holds. At α = .05, the unstandardised slope, standardised β, 95% confidence interval, and adjusted R² will be reported. Because both exposure and willingness are self-selected, the result is an association, not evidence that finfluencers cause risky choices.

## 6. Limitations

Hypothetical willingness may not predict actual investment, and participants may underreport socially undesirable risk. “Finfluencer” covers heterogeneous expertise, sponsorship, and advice. Financial literacy and risk tolerance are difficult to measure briefly, leaving residual confounding; people already attracted to risky assets may seek this content. Convenience recruitment and cross-sectional timing further limit generalisability and causality. A future longitudinal study could record consensually supplied exposure diaries and later self-reported decisions, while independently coding content claims and sponsorship disclosures.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

Omitting risk tolerance and existing investments, defining all investing as “high risk,” or focusing only on the strongest strategy item could portray finfluencers as uniquely reckless. This selective framing would confuse audience selection with influence and distort the outcome.
