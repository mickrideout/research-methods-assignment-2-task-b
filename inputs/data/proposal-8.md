## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, higher scores on the Doomscrolling Scale will positively predict generational blame attribution for the housing crisis, after adjusting for age, household income, and parental home-ownership.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

After adjusting for age, household income, and parental home-ownership, Doomscrolling Scale scores do not predict generational blame attribution for the housing crisis among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** doomscrolling frequency (Doomscrolling Scale, continuous). **DV:** generational blame attribution (7-item continuous scale). **Confounders:** age, household income, and parental home-ownership status.

## 4. Data Collection

Cross-sectional online survey of 130 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university mailing lists, generational-discourse Facebook/Reddit communities, and AUD $150 of advertising. A 10-minute Qualtrics survey administers the 4-item Doomscrolling Scale (Satici et al., 2023), a generational-blame scale, and demographic items. Participants enter a draw for five AUD $80 vouchers (AUD $400), keeping total cost near AUD $550. Informed online consent, voluntary participation, and anonymised data storage meet standard ethics requirements.

## 5. Statistical Tests

Multiple linear regression tests whether doomscrolling score predicts generational blame attribution after adjusting for age, income, and parental home-ownership, appropriate for one continuous outcome with multiple continuous and categorical predictors. Assumptions include linearity, independence of residuals, homoscedasticity, approximate normality of residuals, absence of multicollinearity (VIF < 5), and no undue influence from outliers (Cook's distance). If residual normality is violated, a robust (heteroscedasticity-consistent) standard-error regression will be reported instead. Significance is set at α = .05, with 95% confidence intervals for each coefficient and semi-partial R² as the effect-size measure for doomscrolling. Given the cross-sectional design, results are interpreted as an association, not a causal effect of doomscrolling on blame attribution.

## 6. Limitations

Self-selected sampling from generational-discourse communities may inflate baseline blame attribution and limit generalisability. Both predictor and outcome rely on self-report, risking common-method bias. The cross-sectional design cannot establish temporal precedence between doomscrolling and blame attitudes. Residual confounding from personality traits may remain despite covariate adjustment. A future longitudinal design measuring both variables across multiple waves would clarify directionality.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant coefficient could be reframed as proof doomscrolling 'produces' resentment by omitting income and parental-ownership covariates or emphasising significance over a small semi-partial R². Selectively excluding high-income respondents would inflate the apparent relationship. This is a risk of selective analysis, not recommended practice.
