## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among Australian non-homeowners aged 18–30, political ideology will moderate the association between weekly social-media housing-content exposure and trust in Commonwealth housing-policy responses.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

Among Australian non-homeowners aged 18–30, the exposure-by-ideology interaction will not predict trust in Commonwealth housing-policy responses.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**Predictors:** weekly housing-content minutes, ideology score, and their interaction. **Dependent variable:** mean policy-trust score. **Confounders:** income, education, political interest, general institutional trust, primary platform, and content valence.

## 4. Data Collection

A cross-sectional online survey will recruit 200 Australian residents aged 18–30 who own no home and use social media. University, youth, renter, and politically diverse community channels plus AUD 250 advertising support recruitment. Participants report exposure, a 0–10 ideology placement, four policy-trust items informed by Stone et al. (2020), content valence, and confounders. Feed selection can shape encountered information (Bakshy et al., 2015). Fifteen AUD 40 voucher draws produce about AUD 850 cost and 85 hours. Consent stresses nonpartisanship; responses are anonymous, political items are optional, small cells are protected, and no accounts or posts are collected.

## 5. Statistical Tests

Moderated multiple linear regression will predict continuous policy trust from mean-centred exposure, mean-centred ideology, their product, and prespecified confounders. The interaction coefficient directly tests whether the exposure–trust slope changes across ideology. Diagnostics cover independent observations, linearity, normal and homoscedastic residuals, multicollinearity, and influential cases; heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors will be used if needed. A significant interaction will be probed with estimated slopes at representative ideology values and graphed with 95% confidence bands, not split into arbitrary ideological groups. At α = .05, coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, interaction ΔR², and adjusted R² will be reported. Cross-sectional moderation describes conditional association and does not show that exposure changes trust.

## 6. Limitations

A single left–right item may not capture economic and social ideology separately. Self-reported minutes and valence are imprecise, while politically engaged respondents may be overrepresented. Interaction estimates need larger samples and can be sensitive to influential cases. Unmeasured source credibility or prior policy attitudes could confound associations, and cross-sectional timing permits reverse direction. A future panel could use multidimensional ideology measures, repeated exposure diaries, and baseline trust to test whether exposure precedes change across ideological positions.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

Trying multiple ideology cut-points, reporting only one subgroup slope, or omitting institutional trust could manufacture partisan susceptibility. Even a genuine interaction could be exaggerated by spotlighting significance without ΔR². These choices are selective and should be preregistered.
