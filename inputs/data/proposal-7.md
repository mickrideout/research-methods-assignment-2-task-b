## 1. Proposed Hypothesis

Among non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, randomised exposure to a 20-minute algorithmically curated housing-content feed will cause lower perceived self-efficacy for achieving homeownership than exposure to an equivalent reverse-chronological feed of the same source content.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

There is no difference in post-exposure homeownership self-efficacy between Australians aged 18–30 randomly assigned to an algorithmically curated versus a reverse-chronological housing-content feed.

## 3. Independent, Dependent and Confounding Variables

**IV:** feed type (algorithmic vs chronological, randomly assigned). **DV:** homeownership self-efficacy (validated-style 6-item scale, post-exposure). **Covariates:** baseline self-efficacy, prior platform use, and current income.

## 4. Data Collection

Randomised online experiment with 80 non-home-owning Australians aged 18–30, recruited via university lists and AUD $200 of social-media advertising. A mock feed interface (built with free survey-platform branching) presents identical housing-related posts either algorithmically reordered by engagement cues or in chronological order; participants scroll for 20 minutes before completing a self-efficacy scale. Baseline self-efficacy is measured beforehand. Participants receive a AUD $9 e-voucher each (AUD $720 total), keeping total cost at AUD $920. Informed consent, debriefing, and voluntary withdrawal are provided.

## 5. Statistical Tests

An independent-samples t-test compares post-exposure self-efficacy between the two randomised conditions, matching one categorical, two-group predictor with a continuous, approximately normal outcome. Baseline self-efficacy, prior use, and income are checked for balance and entered as covariates in a supplementary ANCOVA. Assumptions include independence, normality of residuals within groups (Shapiro-Wilk), and homogeneity of variance (Levene's test); Welch's t-test is used if variances differ. Significance is set at α = .05, with a 95% confidence interval for the mean difference and Cohen's d as the effect size. Random assignment supports a causal interpretation of any difference found.

## 6. Limitations

A simulated feed cannot fully replicate real platform algorithms or personalisation history (Li et al., 2026). A single 20-minute session may not capture cumulative, long-term algorithmic effects. Convenience sampling limits generalisability. Demand characteristics may influence self-report scores. A future field study partnering with a platform's research API, if available, could test effects on authentic feeds over a longer period.

## 7. Alternative Narrative

A significant result could be overstated as proof algorithms 'cause' lasting reductions in motivation by ignoring the brief, artificial exposure and omitting baseline self-efficacy as a covariate. Reporting only participants with the largest pre-post drop would exaggerate the effect—a risk of selective analysis, not sound practice.
